Who We Are

About Reshvah

Reshvah Initiative is a youth-led movement working to make sure heritage that is fading doesn't fade unnoticed. We started with silk. We didn't start there because silk is the only thing worth saving — we started there because it's where we happened to be standing.

What we mean by heritage

Heritage can be a craft, a language, a story, a piece of architecture, a dance, a tradition, a community, a memory, or simply a way of life.

रेशम+प्रवाह=रेशवाह

Resham is silk — thread, in its rawest form. Pravah is flow — the way a river, a story, or a skill moves forward across time. Put together, Reshvah is heritage that isn't frozen behind glass, but kept alive by moving: from hand to hand, generation to generation, place to place.

How it started

Reshvah began with an uncomfortable observation. A craft that had survived two centuries, that carries a Geographical Indication tag and reaches export markets, was still struggling to keep its own weavers' children interested in continuing it. A small group of students and young researchers started visiting Bhagalpur's weaving clusters, not to write one more report that would sit unread, but to build something that could actually connect what they documented to people who could act on it. Reshvah Initiative is what came out of that: research-first, honest about what it doesn't know yet, and built to grow as each new city finds a fellow willing to do the same.

🌱 A youth-led initiative

A network, not a headquarters

Bhagalpur is chapter one, not the whole plan. Reshvah isn't a headquarters issuing instructions outward — it's a growing network of research fellows, each anchored to a heritage that matters to them personally, working the same honest way we started in Bhagalpur. What holds the network together isn't a template. It's a shared standard for what "properly documented" means.

Research Fellows — Their City, Their Story

Each new chapter is carried by a research fellow rooted in that place — not a volunteer following a script, but someone documenting a heritage that's already theirs: a craft they grew up watching, a language their grandparents spoke, a building on their own street. A fellow chooses their own city's heritage, does the fieldwork — interviews, photographs, oral histories, records — and tells that story in their own words, the way Bhagalpur's chapter was built. What they send back is raw and personal: a first draft of memory, before anyone else touches it. From there, it's the Research Team's job — not the fellow's — to verify what's been sent, so every chapter that joins the archive is both deeply personal and rigorously true.

Where We're Going

Vision & Mission

Vision
A world where dying heritage crafts don't disappear silently — where the people who still know how to make them have the visibility, dignity, and market access to pass the skill on.
Mission
Reshvah documents Bhagalpur's silk heritage rigorously, connects it to researchers and volunteers worldwide, and builds the awareness and tools needed for its revival — starting in Bhagalpur, with the aim of applying the same model to other endangered heritage over time.
A deliberately wide definition

Heritage can be a craft, a language, a story, a piece of architecture, a dance, a tradition, a community, a memory, or simply a way of life — and our vision reaches beyond Bhagalpur silk to more of it, worldwide.

Our vision isn't bounded by one district or one craft. It's about building a working model — starting here, tested honestly here — carried forward by research fellows who each bring their own city's heritage, in their own words, into a shared, growing archive, checked and verified before it ever gets called truth.

1810–11 · Past

First Documented Account

Francis Buchanan-Hamilton's survey of the district gives one of the earliest written accounts of Tussar silk production around Bhagalpur — the earliest fixed point in this craft's recorded history.

1800s – 1900s · Past

Bhagalpur Becomes "Silk City"

Over more than a century, Tussar and Katan silk weaving grows from a household skill into the district's defining industry, carried forward household by household, loom by loom.

2013 · Present

Geographical Indication Tag

"Bhagalpur Silk" is formally granted its GI tag on 15 January 2013 — official recognition of what weaver families had already known for two centuries.

Today · Present & Crisis

A Living Craft, Under Real Pressure

35,000+ weavers still work roughly 25,000 handlooms, exporting to West Asia, Europe, the US, and Japan. But published research points to a slow squeeze, not a single collapse — the fuller picture is laid out below.

Voices · Present

What Weavers Are Saying

"Yarn costs keep rising and synthetic silk sells for a fraction of the price. It's hard to convince my own children to keep weaving."
Illustrative composite, reflecting weaver interviews described in published research (see sources below)
In Progress · Revival

How Reshvah Is Responding

Documentation and connection, first. An honest, sourced record of the craft's real situation, linked to the researchers, volunteers, and buyers who can actually act on it — starting with this website, and with the people applying to join the Bhagalpur chapter.

Beyond the Timeline

The Crisis, Plainly — and Where Revival Could Begin

The flow above sketches the arc. Here's the fuller, less tidy version — what's actually squeezing the craft, and what a serious response could look like.

⚠️

What's Squeezing the Craft

  • Rising raw yarn costs eating directly into already-thin weaver margins
  • Cheaper synthetic and power-loom silk undercutting handloom prices in the same markets
  • Limited access to formal bank credit for small and household weaving units
  • Middlemen layers that separate weavers from the final selling price of their own work
  • Fewer young people from weaving families choosing to continue it — the quiet gap that led to Reshvah
🌱

What Revival Could Look Like

  • Fair-pricing references so weavers aren't guessing at their own margins
  • Direct-to-buyer channels that cut out layers of markup between loom and customer
  • Documentation and visibility that connects weavers to researchers, buyers, and press who can actually act
  • Careful, weaver-led use of technology — including AI-assisted design and e-commerce — only where it supports the hand rather than replacing it
  • A verified, honest public record, so support goes to what's real rather than what's well-marketed
Sourced from: ODOP Bhagalpur (Govt. of Bihar) · GI Registry, Chennai · Ministry of Textiles, GoI · Buchanan-Hamilton survey, 1810–11 · Kumari & Dutta (2025), Unravelling Threads: The Decline of Bhagalpur Silk Art, Springer · Swaroop & Mani (2025), Bhagalpur Textile Industry, IJAAR Vol. 11, No. 6.
