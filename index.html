Entry No. 01 — Who We Are

About Reshvah

Reshvah Foundation is a youth-led foundation working to make sure heritage that is fading doesn't fade unnoticed. We started with silk. We didn't start there because silk is the only thing worth saving — we started there because it's where we happened to be standing.

What we mean by heritage

Heritage can be a craft, a language, a story, a piece of architecture, a dance, a tradition, a community, a memory, or simply a way of life.

रेशम+प्रवाह=रेशवाह

Resham is silk — thread, in its rawest form. Pravah is flow — the way a river, a story, or a skill moves forward across time. Put together, Reshvah is heritage that isn't frozen behind glass, but kept alive by moving: from hand to hand, generation to generation, place to place.

How it started

Reshvah began with an uncomfortable observation. A craft that had survived two centuries, that carries a Geographical Indication tag and reaches export markets, was still struggling to keep its own weavers' children interested in continuing it. A small group of students and young researchers started visiting Bhagalpur's weaving clusters, not to write one more report that would sit unread, but to build something that could actually connect what they documented to people who could act on it. Reshvah Foundation is what came out of that: research-first, honest about what it doesn't know yet, and built to grow as each new city finds a fellow willing to do the same.

🌱 A youth-led initiative

A network, not a headquarters

Bhagalpur is chapter one, not the whole plan. Reshvah isn't a headquarters issuing instructions outward — it's a growing network of research fellows, each anchored to a heritage that matters to them personally, working the same honest way we started in Bhagalpur. What holds the network together isn't a template. It's a shared standard for what "properly documented" means.

Research Fellows — Their City, Their Story

Each new chapter is carried by a research fellow rooted in that place — not a volunteer following a script, but someone documenting a heritage that's already theirs: a craft they grew up watching, a language their grandparents spoke, a building on their own street. A fellow chooses their own city's heritage, does the fieldwork — interviews, photographs, oral histories, records — and tells that story in their own words, the way Bhagalpur's chapter was built. What they send back is raw and personal: a first draft of memory, before anyone else touches it. From there, it's the Research Team's job — not the fellow's — to verify what's been sent, so every chapter that joins the archive is both deeply personal and rigorously true.

§ 01

Rooted locally

Every chapter starts with real people, real looms, real conversations — not a template applied from elsewhere.

§ 02

Every verified chapter feeds one shared archive — so no single craft's story has to survive in isolation.

Entry No. 02 — Where We're Going

Vision & Mission

Vision
A world where dying heritage crafts don't disappear silently — where the people who still know how to make them have the visibility, dignity, and market access to pass the skill on.
Mission
Reshvah documents Bhagalpur's silk heritage rigorously, connects it to researchers and volunteers worldwide, and builds the awareness and tools needed for its revival — starting in Bhagalpur, with the aim of applying the same model to other endangered heritage over time.
A deliberately wide definition

Heritage can be a craft, a language, a story, a piece of architecture, a dance, a tradition, a community, a memory, or simply a way of life — and our vision reaches beyond Bhagalpur silk to more of it, worldwide.

Our vision isn't bounded by one district or one craft. It's about building a working model — starting here, tested honestly here — carried forward by research fellows who each bring their own city's heritage, in their own words, into a shared, growing archive, checked and verified before it ever gets called truth.

How Reshvah Is Responding

Documentation and connection, first. An honest, sourced record of the craft's real situation, linked to the researchers, volunteers, and buyers who can actually act on it — starting with this website, and with the people applying to join the Bhagalpur chapter.

Beyond the Timeline

The Crisis, Plainly — and Where Revival Could Begin

The flow above sketches the arc. Here's the fuller, less tidy version — what's actually squeezing the craft, and what a serious response could look like.

⚠

What's Squeezing the Craft

  • Rising raw yarn costs eating directly into already-thin weaver margins
  • Cheaper synthetic and power-loom silk undercutting handloom prices in the same markets
  • Limited access to formal bank credit for small and household weaving units
  • Middlemen layers that separate weavers from the final selling price of their own work
  • Fewer young people from weaving families choosing to continue it — the quiet gap that led to Reshvah
✦

What Revival Could Look Like

  • Fair-pricing references so weavers aren't guessing at their own margins
  • Direct-to-buyer channels that cut out layers of markup between loom and customer
  • Documentation and visibility that connects weavers to researchers, buyers, and press who can actually act
  • Careful, weaver-led use of technology — including AI-assisted design and e-commerce — only where it supports the hand rather than replacing it
  • A verified, honest public record, so support goes to what's real rather than what's well-marketed
Bhagalpur Article

The Silken Threads of Bhagalpur: Voices from the Looms

For generations, the rhythmic clack of the loom has been the heartbeat of Bhagalpur, a town synonymous with the rich legacy of silk and chadar weaving. Rooted in history dating back to the legendary era of Chandan Saudagar, this craft was once a booming enterprise, with a local market footprint that commanded an impressive ₹500 crore. Yet, step into the narrow lanes where the weavers reside today, and you will find a starkly different reality—an industry quietly contracting, its annual scale plummeting down to just ₹100 crore.

For the families who have carried this craft forward from father to son, every day is a test of survival. Life on the loom is an uphill battle against rising inflation and shrinking margins. As one weaver describes the daily struggle, “When we cover our head, our feet remain uncovered; when we cover our feet, our head is left exposed.” It is a precarious balancing act just to make ends meet, leaving little to no room for savings.

The day of a weaver is grueling, demanding meticulous labour under harsh conditions. The complex manufacturing workflow is a test of endurance, requiring raw yarn to be dried under the blazing sun before undergoing multi-stage processing like spooling (githiya), bleaching, starching (mali), winding onto bobbins, pattern tracing (tari), and fitting the bharna onto power looms. Even with automated machinery replacing older head-looms and manual setups, the foundational hardships remain unchanged.

At the heart of the crisis is an overwhelming economic squeeze. The price of raw materials has skyrocketed: cotton yarn has jumped to ₹1,300 per bundle, staples have risen significantly, and Tasser silk yarn commands a steep ₹6,000 per kilo. At the same time, the market price of a finished saree has actually dropped from ₹250 to ₹170. With loom production generating a meagre monthly output of about ₹8,000, and daily wages hovering unpredictably between ₹400 and ₹600, owners and workers alike are caught in a financial trap. As costs mount—with domestic gas cylinders surging to nearly ₹1,000 and heavy monthly electricity bills adding to the burden—debt accumulates quickly. “Our owner too suffers from the rising price of suta,” explains a local weaver. “If our wages don't increase too, how can we continue our work in future?”

Compounding these economic pressures is a profound sense of systemic neglect. Artisans point out that Bhagalpur lacks the robust infrastructure, yarn depots, and institutional support seen in major textile hubs like Varanasi, lagging nearly a hundred steps behind in modern market expansion and digital reach. Government schemes are seen as empty assurances offering little tangible relief, while local cash-flow cycles stretch payments across agonizing 15-to-20-day windows. With no local mandi to regulate fair trade, many skilled artisans are forced to abandon their ancestral looms entirely, migrating to cities like Bangalore, Mumbai, Delhi, and Hyderabad just to find basic livelihood.

Behind the celebrated GI tag and historical prestige of Bhagalpur silk lies the quiet erosion of a cultural heritage. These firsthand accounts prove that statistics and economic projections cannot capture the true cost of industrial decline; behind every shrinking crore and rising yarn price are human stories of resilience, exhaustion, and a desperate plea to keep the looms alive.

Recent crisis of Bhagalpur Silk
RECENT CRISIS

Floodwaters Bring Bhagalpur’s Silk Looms to a Standstill

Bhagalpur, Bihar | September 2026

Bhagalpur’s weaving communities are facing severe disruption as the Ganga overflowed into low-lying areas of the city, affecting major weaving hubs including Nathnagar and Champanagar.

For families whose livelihoods depend on handlooms, the flooding has meant more than damaged workspaces. As the water rose, weavers rushed to protect yarn, motors and other valuable loom components. Many looms have remained submerged, raising concerns about lasting mechanical and electrical damage.

The timing has added to the uncertainty. The festive season is traditionally an important period for orders and income, but with production brought to a halt, weavers fear cancelled orders and the loss of several months of earnings.

Beyond the immediate economic impact, prolonged exposure to stagnant floodwater has also created difficult living conditions for families, particularly children and older residents.

For Bhagalpur’s silk-weaving communities, recovery will not end when the water recedes. Damaged equipment will need to be restored, workspaces rebuilt and production restarted at a time when many families are already facing economic pressure.

Why This Matters

Bhagalpur’s silk tradition is not only an industry. It is a living heritage carried forward through generations of weaving families.

When a loom stops, the impact extends beyond a machine. It affects a household’s livelihood, an artisan’s ability to continue their craft, and the continuity of a tradition that forms part of Bhagalpur’s cultural identity.

Reshvah will continue documenting crisis affecting Bhagalpur’s heritage communities and the people who sustain them.

Reported impact: Artisans interviewed in the referenced report describe hundreds of looms being affected by the flooding.

Path One — Research Fellow

Become a Reshvah Research Fellow

Bring the next chapter of heritage to Reshvah.

Reshvah believes that cultural heritage does not exist only in museums or history books. It lives in communities, crafts, traditions, languages, architecture, art forms, stories, practices, and people. There are countless forms of heritage across India and the world that remain undocumented, underrepresented, or increasingly vulnerable. As a Research Fellow, you can identify and document a heritage story from your city or region and help bring it into the Reshvah ecosystem.

What is a Reshvah Research Fellow?

A Research Fellow is a young researcher, student, cultural enthusiast, or heritage-focused individual who identifies an important heritage practice, tradition, craft, art form, site, community, story, or cultural practice in their region and develops it into a potential new Reshvah Chapter. You don't need to be an established researcher. What matters is your curiosity, commitment, ability to research responsibly, and belief that the heritage you discover deserves to be seen, understood, documented, and preserved.

What will a Research Fellow do?

  • Identify an endangered, overlooked, or underrepresented form of cultural heritage in their city or region.
  • Conduct preliminary research and documentation.
  • Speak with relevant communities, practitioners, artists, artisans, historians, researchers, or other knowledge holders where appropriate.
  • Understand the history, present condition, challenges, and significance of the heritage.
  • Identify why the heritage may be declining, endangered, or losing visibility.
  • Develop ideas for how awareness, documentation, research, education, or community engagement could contribute to its revival.
  • Prepare research-based material for consideration as a new Reshvah Chapter.
  • Work with the Reshvah team to develop the chapter and its narrative.
  • Represent the research and heritage story responsibly and respectfully.
What is a "New Chapter"?
"What heritage around us is disappearing before the world notices?" — every Reshvah Chapter begins with this question. A New Chapter is a focused Reshvah initiative dedicated to understanding, documenting, and contributing to the revival of a particular cultural heritage story.

It could focus on a traditional craft, a dying art form, a traditional textile, a local language or dialect, traditional architecture, folk music or dance, oral traditions and stories, indigenous knowledge, traditional food heritage, a cultural community, a historical practice, a heritage site, or another significant form of cultural heritage facing neglect or decline. The Research Fellow does not simply "find a topic" — they help bring a heritage story into the light.

Who can apply?

Students, researchers, writers, cultural enthusiasts, storytellers, creators, heritage enthusiasts, and young people with a genuine interest in cultural preservation can apply. Prior research experience is helpful but not mandatory. What matters most is:

Curiosity. Research. Responsibility. Initiative.

Path Two — Advisory Board

Join the Reshvah Advisory Board

Help shape where Reshvah goes next.

Reshvah is building a long-term movement for cultural heritage revival. To grow responsibly, it needs people who bring experience, knowledge, perspective, credibility, and meaningful networks. The Reshvah Advisory Board is a group of individuals who provide strategic guidance, expertise, mentorship, and perspective to help Reshvah strengthen its work and expand its impact.

What is the Reshvah Advisory Board?

The Advisory Board is a selective, non-executive advisory body that supports Reshvah through knowledge, experience, mentorship, strategic guidance, and connections. Advisors may come from diverse backgrounds, including: cultural heritage, history, archaeology, anthropology, art and design, handloom and handicrafts, academia and research, public policy, journalism and storytelling, social impact, entrepreneurship, technology, education, community development, creative industries, philanthropy, or other fields relevant to Reshvah's mission. The board may include established professionals, academics, researchers, practitioners, creators, entrepreneurs, public figures, or exceptionally experienced young individuals and students whose knowledge, achievements, or perspective can meaningfully contribute to Reshvah.

Who should apply?

The Advisory Board is not intended as a general volunteering position. Applicants should be able to demonstrate a meaningful level of professional or academic experience, subject-matter expertise, leadership, recognized work or achievements, relevant institutional or community experience, strategic perspective, or exceptional experience demonstrating that they can meaningfully contribute to Reshvah's growth. For students or young applicants, selection is based on exceptional demonstrated experience, achievements, expertise, leadership, or contribution — rather than age alone.

How can an Advisory Board member help?

  • Provide strategic guidance to Reshvah.
  • Advise on cultural research and documentation.
  • Mentor Research Fellows.
  • Help evaluate potential Reshvah Chapters.
  • Connect Reshvah with researchers, institutions, practitioners, communities, or organizations.
  • Advise on partnerships and collaborations.
  • Help strengthen the credibility and methodology of Reshvah's work.
  • Provide feedback on major initiatives.
  • Contribute expertise to specific projects.
  • Help Reshvah think beyond its current boundaries.

This role is advisory and voluntary unless otherwise agreed.

Please Note
Applications are reviewed selectively based on relevance, experience, expertise, and the potential contribution to Reshvah's mission.
